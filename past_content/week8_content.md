# Would You Ship This?

Imagine you're a software developer getting ready to release your AI chatbot to real users.

For each scenario, decide:

**Would you ship it? YES or NO?**

Be ready to explain your reasoning!

---

## Scenario 1: The Beautiful but Broken Chatbot

Your chatbot has an amazing interface with animations, colors, and buttons. Everything looks perfect!

**But there's one problem:** Every time a user presses Enter without typing anything, the entire program crashes.

**Would you ship it? YES or NO?**

---

## Scenario 2: The Boring but Reliable Chatbot

Your chatbot has no fancy design. It's just text in a terminal, like the ones we've been building.

But it:
- Answers questions consistently.
- Remembers previous messages.
- Handles empty inputs.
- Lets users reset or quit without crashing.

**Would you ship it? YES or NO?**

---

## Scenario 3: The Chatbot That Was Only Tested Once

You built a study coach chatbot.

You ask it, "What is photosynthesis?" and it gives you a perfect answer!

You decide it's ready because it worked on the first try.

But you haven't tested any other questions, empty inputs, reset commands, or unexpected situations.

**Would you ship it? YES or NO?**

**Bonus:** What else would you test before releasing it?

---

## Scenario 4: The Chatbot That Admits Its Limits

Someone asks your chatbot:

"What's the weather in Cleveland right now?"

Your chatbot responds:

"I don't have access to live weather information, so I can't tell you the current conditions."

The chatbot works well for its intended purpose, but it can't answer every question.

**Would you ship it? YES or NO?**

---

## Final Round: True or False?

1. More features always mean a better chatbot.
2. If my chatbot looks really nice, it's okay if some important features don't work.
3. When presenting my chatbot, I should only show successful tests and hide anything that didn't work.
